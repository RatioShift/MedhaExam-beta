Polan Sarkar: The Man Who Carried Light

Polan Sarkar, whose real name was Harez Uddin, was born on 1 August 1921 in Bagatipara, Natore. His family was poor, but he loved books from his childhood. As a boy, he borrowed books from anyone who would lend them and read them eagerly.

In 1965, he built a school in his village with money he had inherited from his grandfather. In 1990, he introduced a yearly award for the students of the school who stood first in merit. The prize was books. Other students also wished to read, so he lent them books on one condition: they must return them after reading, so that others could borrow them too. Soon the adults of the area also began to borrow books from his collection.

After he was diagnosed with diabetes in 1992, he began to give away books free of charge. He walked from place to place on his own two feet, sharing his love for books. People affectionately called him “Alor Ferrywala”, the one who carries light from door to door. A television programme in 2006 made him known across the country. He received the Ekushey Padak in 2011. He died on 1 March 2019 at his home in Bausha village, Bagha, Rajshahi.

River gypsies, known locally as bedey, are an ethnic group who live on boats. They own no land, so they lead a nomadic life, moving from place to place along the rivers. From May to December they travel in small country boats, which are also their homes. In winter, when the water dries up, they return to the mainland and live in temporary tents on the river banks.

The Sundarbans

The Sundarbans is the largest mangrove forest in the world. It lies in the south-western part of Bangladesh, mostly in the Khulna division, and stretches into India. The forest takes its name from the sundari tree, which grows there in large numbers along with gewa, goran and golpata. It is best known for the Royal Bengal Tiger, but it is also home to spotted deer, crocodiles and hundreds of species of birds. Twice a day, the tides of the sea flood the land and leave it covered with mud, which helps these special trees to grow.

In 1997, UNESCO declared the Sundarbans a World Heritage Site. The Bangladeshi part of the forest covers about 6,000 square kilometres. Fishermen, woodcutters and honey collectors depend on the forest for their living. It also acts as a natural wall that protects our coastal people from cyclones and storm surges. Sadly, rising sea levels, pollution and illegal cutting of trees now threaten it. We must take care of it so that it remains safe for future generations.
